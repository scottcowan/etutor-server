# Dark Patterns — How Apps and Websites Are Designed to Trick You

## 100 — Introduction

A "dark pattern" is when an app or website is deliberately designed to make you do something you didn't really mean to do — like making the "cancel subscription" button tiny and grey while the "keep subscription" button is big and bright, or hiding the real cost until the very last step of checkout.

## 200 — Developing

Common dark patterns have names: "roach motel" (easy to get in, hard to get out — like cancelling a subscription), "confirmshaming" (guilt-tripping wording on a decline button, like "No thanks, I don't want to save money"), "drip pricing" (hiding fees until the final step), and "forced continuity" (a free trial that silently becomes a paid subscription unless you actively cancel). Designer Harry Brignull coined the term "dark patterns" in 2010 and built a public gallery of real examples from major companies.

## 300 — Intermediate

Regulators have begun treating dark patterns as a legal, not just ethical, problem: the EU's Digital Services Act and various US state laws now specifically prohibit certain manipulative interface designs, and the US FTC has fined companies for subscription cancellation flows designed to be deliberately confusing. Dark patterns exploit well-documented cognitive biases (loss aversion, default bias, decision fatigue) — meaning the same psychology research that explains nudge theory's benevolent uses also explains how the identical techniques can be turned manipulative.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level human-computer interaction research classifies dark patterns by mechanism (e.g. "sneaking," "urgency," "obstruction," "social proof," "forced action" — a taxonomy developed by Gray, Kou, Battles, Hoggatt, and Toombs in 2018) and studies which patterns are most effective against which cognitive vulnerabilities. A live academic and regulatory debate concerns where genuinely persuasive design (making a good default easy to choose) ends and manipulative design (making an informed choice difficult) begins — the same design toolkit is used for both, and intent is often only provable through internal company documents, echoing the evidentiary challenges seen in tobacco and pharmaceutical manipulation cases.
