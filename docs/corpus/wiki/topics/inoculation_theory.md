# Inoculation — How Forewarning Protects Against Manipulation

## 100 — Introduction

Just like a vaccine can help your body resist a real illness by exposing it to a weakened version first, "inoculation theory" says you can help your mind resist manipulation by showing it a weakened, clearly-labelled example of the trick beforehand — so when you meet the real thing, you recognise it.

## 200 — Developing

This is sometimes called "prebunking" (as opposed to debunking, which happens after the fact): rather than correcting a lie once someone already believes it, prebunking warns people about a manipulation technique before they encounter it, so they're primed to notice and resist it. Studies have shown short "prebunking" videos explaining common misinformation techniques (like fake experts or scapegoating) measurably improved people's ability to spot those techniques in unrelated content afterward.

## 300 — Intermediate

Psychologist William McGuire developed inoculation theory in the 1960s, originally studying resistance to persuasion in advertising and political contexts, decades before it was applied to misinformation specifically. Modern research (notably by Sander van der Linden and colleagues at Cambridge) has built browser games and short videos (like *Bad News* and *Harmony Square*) that let people practise spotting manipulation techniques in a safe, simulated environment — an approach shown in peer-reviewed studies to measurably reduce susceptibility to real misinformation afterward.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level inoculation research distinguishes "fact-based" inoculation (pre-teaching the correct facts on a specific topic) from "technique-based" or "logic-based" inoculation (teaching the manipulation technique itself, which generalises across many different false claims that use the same technique) — the technique-based approach is generally found to be more durable and broadly applicable. Debate continues over how long inoculation effects last without "booster" reinforcement, and whether it works as well against emotionally-charged identity-based misinformation as it does against more neutral factual claims.
