# Censorship — What Governments Have Suppressed and Why

## 100 — Introduction

Censorship is when a government or powerful group stops people from reading, watching, or saying certain things. Governments have banned books, newspapers, films, and websites throughout history — sometimes to prevent real harm, but very often just to protect their own power.

## 200 — Developing

Book banning has a long history: from the Catholic Church's *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* (started 1559, listing forbidden books) to Nazi Germany's 1933 public book burnings targeting Jewish and dissident authors, to modern school and library book bans in the US and UK today. Wartime press censorship — controlling what journalists can report about ongoing conflicts — is treated differently in most democracies from peacetime censorship of ideas, though the line between the two is often contested.

## 300 — Intermediate

The UK's Official Secrets Act and D-Notice system historically restricted what British media could report on national security matters, while apartheid-era South Africa and the Soviet Union both used extensive, formal state censorship bureaucracies to control media narratives. Comparing censorship regimes shows a pattern: authoritarian systems tend to censor pre-emptively and broadly (banning entire topics), while democracies more often use narrower, contestable restrictions (defamation law, classified information rules) that can be challenged in court.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level media history distinguishes censorship (suppressing existing content) from prior restraint (blocking publication before it happens) — a legal distinction central to landmark UK and US press freedom cases. Scholars also study "soft censorship" — economic pressure, defamation lawsuits designed to bankrupt critics (SLAPP suits), and algorithmic deprioritisation on platforms — as forms of suppression that don't require an explicit government ban to achieve a similar chilling effect on speech.
