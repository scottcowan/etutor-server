# The Same Playbook — From Tobacco to Climate to Opioids

## 100 — Introduction

The tricks that tobacco companies used to hide the truth about cigarettes were later copied almost exactly by other industries — for climate change, and for opioid painkillers. It's the same playbook, reused.

## 200 — Developing

Once you learn the tobacco pattern — fund friendly research, demand "balance," attack the credibility of inconvenient scientists, delay regulation for as long as possible — you start recognising it everywhere. Climate change denial in the 1990s–2000s and the opioid crisis of the 2000s–2010s both followed remarkably similar sequences, sometimes even using the same PR firms and expert witnesses as the tobacco defense.

## 300 — Intermediate

Purdue Pharma's marketing of OxyContin as "less addictive" echoed the tobacco industry's "no proof of harm" messaging almost word for word, and internal Purdue documents released through litigation showed executives were aware of high addiction rates while continuing aggressive sales targets. Exxon's own scientists produced accurate internal climate models in the 1970s–80s while the company's public communications sowed doubt about warming for the following two decades — a pattern investigative journalists later called "Exxon knew."

## 400 — Advanced / University

Comparative historians of science treat "merchants of doubt" as a repeatable institutional technology, not a one-off scandal: the same small set of think tanks, contrarian scientists-for-hire, and legal strategies (challenge causation, demand certainty standards impossible for any science to meet, exploit media's need for "both sides") recur across tobacco, acid rain, ozone, climate, and opioids. Recognising the pattern is itself a media-literacy skill — once a child can name the playbook, they can spot it applied to a new industry before the litigation and leaked memos eventually confirm it decades later.
