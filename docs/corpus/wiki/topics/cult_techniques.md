# Cult Techniques — How Groups Capture and Control Members

## 100 — Introduction

A cult is a group that uses specific, well-documented techniques to take control of members' beliefs and decisions — often gradually, so members don't notice it happening. It usually starts with something that feels genuinely good, like intense friendship and belonging.

## 200 — Developing

"Love bombing" — overwhelming a new member with attention, praise, and warmth very early on — is one of the most common recruitment techniques, creating a powerful sense of belonging before any demanding beliefs or behaviours are introduced. Isolation from family and old friends, controlling information (limiting access to outside news or criticism), and a rigid us-vs-them worldview are then introduced gradually, once the emotional bond is already strong.

## 300 — Intermediate

Psychiatrist Robert Lifton's research on Chinese "thought reform" (coercive persuasion) in the 1950s identified eight specific techniques still used to describe cult control today, including "milieu control" (controlling the person's total environment and information) and "loading the language" (using in-group jargon that reshapes how members think, making outside ideas literally harder to articulate). Former members of documented groups (e.g. Jonestown, NXIVM, Scientology) have given detailed public testimony matching this same technique set closely enough that psychologists treat it as a genuinely identifiable pattern, not group-specific coincidence.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level cult psychology distinguishes coercive persuasion from ordinary intense religious or ideological commitment using specific structural markers: does the group permit exit without severe social/financial punishment, does it tolerate internal dissent, does leadership claim unique, unquestionable authority. Steven Hassan's BITE model (Behaviour, Information, Thought, Emotional control) is a widely used academic and clinical framework for assessing the degree of undue influence present in any group, cult or otherwise, and is applied by researchers studying both fringe religious movements and coercive high-control organisations more broadly.
