# Radicalisation — How People Are Drawn to Extreme Ideas

## 100 — Introduction

Radicalisation is the gradual process by which someone moves from ordinary views toward extreme ones — it usually happens step by step, not overnight, and often starts with a real feeling (like loneliness or unfairness) that extreme groups offer to fix.

## 200 — Developing

Online radicalisation pipelines often follow a pattern: a person searches for content on a personal struggle (loneliness, anger at a situation, a search for identity or purpose), a recommendation algorithm optimised for engagement serves progressively more extreme content because extreme content holds attention, and online communities provide the belonging and validation that made the person receptive in the first place. Researchers call the sequence of communities that gradually escalate content extremity a "pipeline" precisely because each step feels like a small, reasonable move from the last.

## 300 — Intermediate

Studies of the "alt-right pipeline" on platforms like YouTube documented how recommendation algorithms could move viewers from mainstream political commentary toward increasingly extreme content over a series of automated suggestions, prompting platforms to adjust recommendation systems specifically in response. Radicalisation researchers emphasise that most people who consume some extreme content do not go on to commit violence — the pipeline concept describes a risk pathway and vulnerability factors (social isolation, grievance, need for identity), not a deterministic outcome.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level terrorism and extremism studies use frameworks like the "staircase to terrorism" (psychologist Fathali Moghaddam) to model radicalisation as a series of increasingly narrow, harder-to-reverse choices rather than a single event, and compare online radicalisation pipelines directly against offline cult recruitment techniques, finding substantial technique overlap (love bombing equivalents, information control, us-vs-them framing) despite very different ideological content. Prevention research (including the UK's Prevent programme, which remains contested in effectiveness and civil-liberties debates) generally targets the vulnerability factors and pipeline mechanics rather than attempting to counter specific ideological content directly, since the psychological pathway is largely ideology-agnostic.
