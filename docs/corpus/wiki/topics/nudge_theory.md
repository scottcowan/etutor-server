# Nudge Theory — How Environments Shape Choices Without You Noticing

## 100 — Introduction

A "nudge" changes the choices around you to make one option easier, without removing any other option or forcing you. Putting fruit at eye level in a school cafeteria instead of hiding it behind the sweets is a nudge — you can still choose sweets, but the easy option changed.

## 200 — Developing

Nudge theory rests on the finding that people are heavily influenced by "defaults" — whatever happens automatically if you do nothing — because changing a default requires active effort most people don't bother with. Organ donation is a well-known real example: countries where you must opt out of donation (rather than opt in) have dramatically higher donor registration rates, purely because of which choice is the default, not because attitudes toward donation differ.

## 300 — Intermediate

Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's 2008 book *Nudge* formalised this as "libertarian paternalism" — the idea that governments and institutions can steer people toward better choices (saving more for retirement, eating healthier) while preserving full freedom to choose otherwise. The UK government created a dedicated "Behavioural Insights Team" (nicknamed the "Nudge Unit") in 2010, one of the first formal government units anywhere built specifically to apply behavioural economics to public policy.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level behavioural economics distinguishes a nudge (preserves choice, transparent, low-cost to opt out) from a "sludge" (deliberately added friction that makes a beneficial choice harder, often used manipulatively) and debates where legitimate choice architecture ends and manipulation begins — precisely the same boundary question raised by dark patterns, just applied to policy rather than commercial UX. Thaler received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Economics largely for this body of work, but critics (including some behavioural economists themselves) argue nudging can function as a low-cost substitute for harder structural policy changes, nudging individual behaviour instead of addressing systemic causes.
