# How Pharmaceutical Companies Market Diseases

## 100 — Introduction

Sometimes a drug company doesn't just advertise a medicine — it helps convince people that an ordinary experience is actually a medical condition that needs treating. This is sometimes called "disease mongering," and it's a real marketing strategy, not an accident.

## 200 — Developing

Drug companies fund advertising, patient advocacy groups, and even medical conferences that raise awareness of a condition right around the time they launch a new drug to treat it. Restless leg syndrome, low testosterone ("low T"), and social anxiety disorder all saw major public-awareness pushes that closely tracked new drug launches rather than new scientific discoveries about how common these conditions actually were.

## 300 — Intermediate

The opioid crisis is the most consequential case study: Purdue Pharma trained sales representatives to tell doctors that OxyContin carried a "less than 1% addiction risk," a figure with no solid evidence behind it, while paying doctors as "key opinion leaders" to promote aggressive prescribing at medical conferences. The FDA approved OxyContin's label based partly on Purdue-funded studies, illustrating how regulatory capture (industry influence over the body meant to police it) let a knowingly risky marketing claim become official guidance.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level pharmaceutical marketing analysis distinguishes "disease mongering" (expanding the definition of illness) from "off-label promotion" (illegally marketing a drug for unapproved uses) and "publication bias" (funding studies likely to show positive results and shelving the ones that don't). Purdue Pharma's 2020 criminal guilty plea and multi-billion-dollar settlements are studied alongside tobacco's Master Settlement Agreement as parallel case studies in how litigation, not initial regulation, eventually surfaces the internal evidence of intent.
