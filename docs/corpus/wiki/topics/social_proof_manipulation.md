# Social Proof and Authority — How We're Manipulated by "Everyone Else"

## 100 — Introduction

"Social proof" means we tend to think something must be good or true if lots of other people already believe it or are doing it. This is often a sensible shortcut — but people who want to manipulate us can fake it, making something look more popular or more official than it really is.

## 200 — Developing

Fake five-star reviews, purchased social media followers, laugh tracks on TV comedy shows, and "as seen on TV" style endorsements all exploit social proof the same way — by manufacturing the appearance that other people already approve. "Authority" bias works similarly: a person in a white coat, a uniform, or with an impressive title is trusted more readily, even on topics unrelated to their actual expertise, which is why adverts often feature actors dressed as doctors or scientists.

## 300 — Intermediate

Psychologist Robert Cialdini's research (formalised in his 1984 book *Influence*) identified social proof and authority as two of six core principles of persuasion, alongside reciprocity, commitment/consistency, liking, and scarcity — research originally conducted by studying professional salespeople, con artists, and fundraisers to reverse-engineer what actually works. Stanley Milgram's famous 1961 obedience experiments demonstrated how far ordinary people would go against their own conscience simply because a person in a lab coat, presented as a scientific authority, instructed them to continue.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level social psychology distinguishes "informational social influence" (genuinely believing the crowd has information you lack) from "normative social influence" (conforming just to avoid social disapproval, even while privately disagreeing) — Solomon Asch's 1950s conformity experiments demonstrated the second effect starkly, showing participants would give an answer they knew was wrong rather than contradict a unanimous group. This research underpins both cult recruitment techniques and online radicalisation pipelines, which deliberately construct environments of manufactured unanimous "consensus" to accelerate belief change.
