# Who Owns the Media — And Why It Matters

## 100 — Introduction

A small number of very large companies own most of the newspapers, TV channels, and websites we get news from. Who owns a news outlet can affect which stories get covered, and how — even without anyone directly ordering a specific lie.

## 200 — Developing

In the UK, Rupert Murdoch's News UK owns The Sun and The Times, and his broader global company News Corp/Fox has been extremely influential in both UK and US politics for decades. Media consolidation — a small number of owners controlling an increasing share of outlets — has been a long-term trend across most Western democracies since the 1980s.

## 300 — Intermediate

Media ownership can shape coverage in subtle ways: which stories get prominent placement, which get ignored, which experts get invited onto panels, and how editorial endorsements at election time are decided — the 1992 UK general election headline "It's The Sun Wot Won It" became a case study in how a single owner's editorial position could plausibly be claimed to have shifted an election outcome. Cross-ownership rules (limiting how much of the same market one company can own) exist in many countries specifically to prevent any single owner's viewpoint from dominating public information.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level media economics research (building on Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's 1988 "propaganda model") argues that ownership concentration, advertiser dependence, and reliance on official government/corporate sources create systemic biases in what counts as "newsworthy" — without requiring any individual editor to consciously distort a story. Scholars debate whether digital-era fragmentation (thousands of independent online outlets, newsletters, and creators) has weakened traditional ownership concentration's influence, or whether algorithmic distribution has simply created new, less visible gatekeepers (platform recommendation systems) with similar concentrated influence.
