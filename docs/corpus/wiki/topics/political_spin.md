# Political Spin — How Politicians Frame Reality

## 100 — Introduction

"Spin" is when someone chooses their words very carefully to make a fact sound better or worse than it really is — without technically lying. A tax rise might be called "asking the wealthy to contribute a bit more," while the exact same policy from an opponent's mouth might be called "a raid on your paycheck."

## 200 — Developing

Political spin relies heavily on framing — the same underlying fact, phrased differently, changes how people feel about it. Modern political parties employ "spin doctors" (professional communications strategists) whose entire job is choosing which facts to emphasise, which words to use, and how to respond to bad news quickly enough to control the story before journalists frame it themselves.

## 300 — Intermediate

The term "spin doctor" entered common British political usage in the 1990s, closely associated with the rise of professionalised press offices like Alastair Campbell's role under Tony Blair's government. Linguist George Lakoff's research on political framing (e.g. "tax relief" implying tax is inherently a burden to be relieved) shows that word choice can shape a listener's underlying assumptions even when no factual claim is being made at all.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level political communication studies distinguish "spin" (selective emphasis and framing of true information) from disinformation (deliberately false information) — spin is generally legal and normalised in democratic politics, while the two techniques often overlap in practice and the public frequently cannot tell them apart in real time. Scholars analyse spin using framing theory and agenda-setting theory, examining how repeated media framing over months or years can shift public opinion more durably than any single persuasive statement.
