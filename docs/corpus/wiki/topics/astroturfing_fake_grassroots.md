# Astroturfing — Fake Grassroots Movements

## 100 — Introduction

"Grassroots" means a movement that starts with ordinary people, from the ground up. "Astroturfing" is when a company or political group fakes that — paying people to pretend a movement is spontaneous and popular when really it was organised and funded from the top by someone with something to gain.

## 200 — Developing

The name comes from AstroTurf, the brand of fake grass — the joke being that astroturfing looks like a real grassroots movement from a distance but isn't natural underneath. Techniques include paying actors to attend rallies, creating fake local citizen groups with reassuring names, hiring people to write "independent" letters to newspapers or leave positive reviews, and using bot or sockpuppet social media accounts to make an opinion look more widely held than it is.

## 300 — Intermediate

A well-documented case: tobacco and later vaping companies funded groups with names suggesting grassroots consumer advocacy that in fact lobbied against public health regulations on their sponsors' behalf. Astroturfing exploits a real psychological shortcut called social proof (we assume something popular is more likely correct or worth supporting) — which is exactly why manufacturing the appearance of popularity is worth the expense to whoever is funding it.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level political communication research studies astroturfing alongside "sockpuppetry" (one person or organisation controlling many fake identities) and coordinated inauthentic behaviour on social platforms, which platform trust-and-safety teams and academic researchers now detect using network analysis (identifying accounts that post in suspiciously synchronised patterns). Legal responses vary by country: some jurisdictions require political advertising to disclose its true funder, but enforcement is inconsistent and astroturfing campaigns can often route funding through several layers of shell organisations to obscure the original source.
