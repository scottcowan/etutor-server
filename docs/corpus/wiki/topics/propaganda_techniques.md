# Propaganda Techniques — The Specific Methods, Named

## 100 — Introduction

Propaganda is a message designed to make you feel or believe something without giving you the full picture. There are specific, well-known tricks it uses — like showing "everyone else agrees" (bandwagon), or using warm, vague words that sound good but mean little (glittering generalities).

## 200 — Developing

Named techniques include: bandwagon (join in because everyone else is), glittering generality (vague positive words like "freedom" or "progress" used without specifics), transfer (borrowing the authority or emotion of a symbol, like a flag, for an unrelated message), plain folks (a powerful person acting ordinary to seem relatable), and fear appeal (making a threat feel urgent and personal to bypass careful thinking). These were catalogued formally by the US Institute for Propaganda Analysis in the 1930s and are still the standard checklist media-literacy teachers use today.

## 300 — Intermediate

Edward Bernays, often called the "father of public relations," deliberately applied his uncle Sigmund Freud's ideas about unconscious desire to advertising and political persuasion in the 1920s, most famously engineering the 1929 "Torches of Freedom" campaign that linked women's smoking to the suffrage movement to grow a new market for cigarettes. His 1928 book *Propaganda* argued openly that a modern society requires an "invisible government" of persuasion professionals to manage public opinion — a claim now studied as a founding document of the entire persuasion industry.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level propaganda studies distinguish "white" propaganda (true information deployed for persuasive advantage), "grey" propaganda (source deliberately unclear), and "black" propaganda (deliberately false, often attributed to an enemy to discredit them) — a taxonomy developed extensively during WWII. Jacques Ellul's *Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes* (1962) argues that modern mass-media propaganda works less through single persuasive messages and more through constant, ambient repetition that shapes what feels "normal" to believe, independent of any specific argument's logical strength.
