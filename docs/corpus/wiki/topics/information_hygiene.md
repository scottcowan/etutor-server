# Information Hygiene — How to Build a Healthy Information Diet

## 100 — Introduction

Just like eating a balanced diet keeps your body healthy, choosing where your news and information comes from — and in what mix — keeps your thinking healthy. "Information hygiene" means building good habits for what sources you trust, how many different ones you check, and how you react to surprising claims.

## 200 — Developing

Good information hygiene habits include following a small number of sources you've actually checked for reliability rather than whatever an algorithm surfaces, treating a single unverified claim (especially an emotionally charged one) with extra caution until you've seen it independently confirmed, and periodically checking who funds or owns the outlets you rely on. This is the practical, ongoing-habit counterpart to recognising_manipulation's in-the-moment skill — one is what you do when a specific claim appears, the other is how you structure your everyday information environment before that moment arrives.

## 300 — Intermediate

Fact-checking organisations (e.g. Full Fact in the UK, the International Fact-Checking Network globally) publish their verification methodology publicly, and studying that methodology — checking primary sources, contacting the original claim-maker, distinguishing correlation from causation — is itself a transferable information hygiene skill, not just a way to outsource judgement to fact-checkers. Echo chambers (environments where you mostly encounter agreement, whether by choice or algorithmic curation) are one of the biggest threats to information hygiene, because they remove the natural friction of encountering disagreement that would otherwise prompt double-checking a claim.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level media literacy research frames information hygiene as analogous to public health: an individual's habits matter, but so does the surrounding information "environment" (platform design, algorithmic curation, regulatory transparency requirements) — much like personal hygiene alone can't fully protect someone in a genuinely unsanitary environment. Researchers studying "digital literacy interventions" find that habit-based approaches (diversifying sources, practising lateral reading, pausing before sharing) tend to produce more durable behaviour change than one-off fact-correction campaigns, which connects this topic directly back to inoculation theory's technique-based (not fact-based) approach.
