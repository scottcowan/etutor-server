# Big Tobacco — How an Industry Lied for Fifty Years and Got Away With It

## 100 — Introduction

A big tobacco company found out, from its own scientists, that smoking caused cancer and was addictive — and then kept selling cigarettes anyway while telling the public there was "no proof" it was harmful. They did this for decades. It is one of the most well-documented cases of a company choosing profit over telling the truth.

## 200 — Developing

From the 1950s onward, tobacco companies' own internal research confirmed the cancer and addiction links, but their public message stayed the opposite: "more research is needed." Rather than hide the science entirely, they funded a rival stream of studies designed to produce confusion rather than clarity, and used friendly scientists and PR firms to make doubt look like an honest scientific debate.

## 300 — Intermediate

The strategy had a name inside the industry, revealed in a leaked 1969 Brown & Williamson memo: "Doubt is our product." Courts in the 1990s forced the release of millions of internal documents (the "Tobacco Papers") showing decades of coordinated funding of pro-tobacco research, lobbying against regulation, and targeted marketing to teenagers and vulnerable groups, even as executives testified under oath that nicotine was not addictive.

## 400 — Advanced / University

The 1998 Master Settlement Agreement between US states and major tobacco companies (worth over $200 billion) followed the largest civil litigation settlement in US history and forced public release of the internal documents now archived at the UCSF Truth Tobacco Industry Documents library. Historians of science (notably Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway's *Merchants of Doubt*) use the tobacco case as the founding template for studying how corporations manufacture scientific uncertainty as a delay tactic — a playbook later reused by the fossil fuel and opioid industries.
