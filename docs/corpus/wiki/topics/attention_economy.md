# The Attention Economy — You Are the Product

## 100 — Introduction

Many apps and websites are free to use, but that doesn't mean they're free — they make money by keeping your attention and showing you adverts, or by collecting data about what you like so advertisers can target you. The longer you stay, the more money the app makes, so the app is designed to keep you scrolling.

## 200 — Developing

Social media feeds use recommendation algorithms trained to maximise "engagement" (time spent, likes, shares, comments) — and content that provokes strong emotion (especially outrage, fear, or novelty) tends to be more engaging than calm, accurate content, so algorithms often surface it more, whether or not anyone designed them to be biased that way. Features like infinite scroll, autoplay, and notification badges were specifically engineered using research from behavioural psychology and slot-machine design to make stopping feel harder than continuing.

## 300 — Intermediate

The phrase "if you're not paying for the product, you are the product" captures the business model called surveillance capitalism (a term coined by scholar Shoshana Zuboff): platforms collect behavioural data as raw material, refine it into predictions about what will hold your attention or what you'll buy, and sell those predictions to advertisers. Former tech insiders (including designers who worked on early Facebook and Google features) have since publicly described specific persuasive-design decisions they now regret, citing psychologist B.F. Skinner's variable-reward research (the same mechanism behind gambling addiction) as a direct influence on notification and feed design.

## 400 — Advanced / University

University-level attention economy research examines how the shift from a scarcity-of-content economy (few TV channels, limited papers) to an abundance-of-content economy (infinite feeds) inverted the scarce resource from content to human attention itself, changing the fundamental incentives of media businesses. Scholars debate whether regulation (e.g. the EU's Digital Services Act provisions on recommender-system transparency) can meaningfully counter attention-economy incentives, or whether the profit motive is so deeply structural that only a different business model (subscription, public funding) removes the incentive entirely.
