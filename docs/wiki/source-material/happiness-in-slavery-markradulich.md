## Core Thesis

Control doesn't need to make people miserable to persist. It succeeds by delivering real psychological and material rewards — order, safety, belonging, relief from responsibility. People don't choose authority because they've abandoned freedom; they choose it because freedom has come to feel like disorder, impotence, and exhausting responsibility.

## Structure

### Act 1 — *They Live* (1988, John Carpenter): Why people can't see the system

The key scene isn't the alien reveal — it's the six-minute alley fight between Nada and Frank. Frank doesn't defend the aliens (he doesn't know they exist). He defends his *right not to know*. Seeing the truth would impose a responsibility to act. Before the glasses, he's another worker trying to survive. Afterward, every ordinary decision risks becoming collaboration.

The film's more sophisticated argument runs through the human collaborators — people who *do* know the system and cooperate because proximity to power is preferable to solidarity with the ruled. This is the psychology of **social dominance orientation**: some people aren't afraid of the boot; they object only to being beneath it.

**Bridge to the present:** OECD survey data — 90% of US firms use at least one form of algorithmic management. Software assigns schedules, tracks speed and location, sets targets, rewards and sanctions. The boss was absorbed into the machine. A human supervisor can be questioned, embarrassed, persuaded, or held responsible. An algorithm produces a score. Domination is accepted because it also promises consistency, efficiency, and freedom from human favouritism. Control sold as objective management.

### Act 2 — *V for Vendetta* (2005): Why people accept control even once they can see it

Norsefire didn't seize power against the population's will — it restored order after war, disease, and social collapse. Frightened populations don't initially surrender liberty for the pleasure of obedience. They surrender for food distribution, public order, protection from violence, the promise that children will survive. Each concession appears temporary. By the time the emergency has become the permanent structure of political life, obedience has acquired the moral status of *responsibility*.

**The revolutionary's contradiction:** V's treatment of Evey mirrors the state's. V imprisons her, shaves her head, starves her, and engineers a fake execution. He claims to have liberated her from fear. But V terrorises Evey to manufacture freedom the same way the state terrorises to manufacture obedience. Both believe they have the right to break an individual to produce the kind of person their political system requires. Both claim superior understanding of what the controlled person needs. The direction of coercion does not change its nature.

**Survey data:**
- YouGov: 52% of UK adults aged 13–27 wanted a "strong leader unconstrained by parliament or elections"
- King's College London follow-up: reworded as "dictator" → dropped to 6%

People want the *fantasy* of authority — decisive action directed at someone else. The imagined strong man bypasses parliament to build homes, punish criminals, control borders — he does not censor the respondent, seize their property, or imprison their family. Control remains attractive because it is imagined as exercised *on behalf of* ordinary people, against somebody else.

**El Salvador/Bukele:** Dramatic reduction in gang violence under a state of emergency that has suspended constitutional rights since 2022. Human Rights Watch documents mass arbitrary detention, disappearances, torture, due process violations, removal of presidential term limits. But the declining gang violence is real. For someone who can now walk through their neighbourhood without being extorted or murdered, procedural rights feel abstract beside the practical freedom to leave home. The choice is not experienced as freedom vs slavery — it's experienced as freedom from state coercion vs freedom from criminal coercion.

### Act 3 — *Y: The Last Man* (FX series): The material foundation beneath it all

Every mammal with a Y chromosome dies. Aircraft fall because pilots die. Power plants lose experienced operators. Trucking, construction, oil production, industrial agriculture suffer catastrophic labour shortages.

The show's premise makes labour visible. Electricity ordinarily appears to come from the wall, food from the store. Modern economies conceal the human coordination beneath their products. The disappearance of that coordination reveals civilisation as a fragile network of people who know how to do a particular thing at particular times.

**The question revolutionary fiction postpones:** *They Live* can destroy the transmitter. *V for Vendetta* can destroy parliament. Neither explosion teaches anyone to operate an electrical grid. Demolition has a villain, a symbol, and a visible moment of victory. Maintenance has shift schedules.

Government is not only a president, parliament, or ideological apparatus. It is also maintenance, logistics, succession planning, and the preservation of expertise.

Yorick's failure as a character: the show treats the apocalypse as background for an individual adventure rather than the collapse of coordinated life. It reproduces the cultural tendency it should have examined.

## Full Transcript (Whisper base model, local transcription)

> The most unsettling thing about slavery is not that human beings can be forced into it. We have known that for as long as one tribe has been able to overpower another. The more enduring insight is contained in the title of the Nine Inch Nails song, Happiness in Slavery. Domination can offer psychological rewards to the dominated. Control can feel like safety, obedience can feel like belonging, and the surrender of choice can feel like relief. People do not always choose authority because they have ceased valuing freedom. They choose it because freedom has become associated with disorder, impotence, and the exhausting responsibility of governing a world they no longer understand.
>
> They Live, V for Vendetta, and the television adaptation of Y: The Last Man trace three stages of this transaction. They Live asked why people cannot see the system controlling them. V for Vendetta asks why people accept that control once they can see it. Y: The Last Man supplies the answer the first two stories largely avoid. When the systems supporting ordinary life fail, people become less interested in the moral purity of authority than in whether anyone knows how to turn the electricity back on.
>
> The strong man's enduring appeal is that he promises to transform helplessness into order. The people seeking dominance receive power. The people accepting domination receive relief.
>
> John Carpenter makes the control in They Live deliberately crude. Billboards commanding people to obey, reproduce, and consume are hidden beneath advertising. Money tells its holder that it is his God. Newspapers conceal the same commands beneath the appearance of information. The ruling aliens have not abolished personal choice. They have arranged society so that obedience is experienced as choice. People shop, work, watch television, and pursue status without understanding that their desires have been organised for them.
>
> Nada's sunglasses destroy that arrangement. Their purpose is not merely to expose the aliens. They make normal life impossible. Once Nada can read the commands, he can no longer experience consumption as innocent or authority as legitimate. This is why the six-minute alley fight with Frank matters, despite its absurd length. The film's most exhausting battle is not between humanity and its alien rulers. It is between two construction workers over whether one of them will look at reality. Frank does not defend the aliens. He does not know they exist. What he defends is his right not to know. He has found employment, shelter, and a fragile place inside the existing order. Putting on the glasses would not simply give him new information. It would impose a responsibility to act upon it. Before he sees, he is another worker trying to survive. Afterward, every ordinary decision risks becoming collaboration.
>
> Carpenter's political fantasy is that exploitation persists because the population cannot perceive it. Yet the film contains a more sophisticated explanation in its human collaborators. They know the aliens are stripping the planet and degrading everyone beneath them. They cooperate because the arrangement gives them money, status, and access. One former resident of the homeless camp reappears inside the alien facility, delighted with his promotion. He has not been hypnotised. He has calculated that proximity to the ruling class is preferable to solidarity with the ruled.
>
> This is the psychology of social dominance. Some people view society as a competition among groups in which equality is either impossible or undesirable. They are drawn to hierarchy because they expect coercion to flow downward. The authoritarian state does not frighten them because they imagine themselves as its officials, loyal citizens, or protected insiders rather than targets. They do not object to the boot. They object to being beneath it.
>
> That bargain has become less theatrical and more automated. An OECD survey of more than 6,000 firms found that 90% of American businesses surveyed used at least one form of algorithmic management to instruct, monitor, or evaluate workers. Software now assigns schedules, tracks speed and location, evaluates communication, sets targets, rewards performance, and sanctions failure. Managers often believe these systems improve decision-making, even as many reports concerned about opaque logic, unclear accountability, and harm to workers. The boss has not disappeared. He has been absorbed into the machinery. A human supervisor can be questioned and embarrassed, persuaded, or held responsible. An algorithm produces a score. The worker is not chained to the machine. The machine tells him when to work, measures whether he worked correctly, and decides whether he may return tomorrow. Its domination is accepted because it also promises consistency, efficiency, and freedom from human favouritism. Control is not sold as control. It is sold as objective management.
>
> V for Vendetta begins where They Live ends. Its population does not need special glasses to recognise that it lives beneath an authoritarian government. Norsefire rules openly through surveillance, censorship, secret police, and terror. Its legitimacy comes from having restored order after war, disease, and social collapse. The British people did not awaken one morning and decide they hated freedom. They accepted a government that appeared capable of ending catastrophe.
>
> This is the historical route by which authoritarianism usually becomes respectable. Frightened populations do not initially surrender liberty for the pleasure of obedience. They surrender for food distribution, public order, protection from violence, and the promise that their children will survive. Each concession appears temporary and rational. The government asks for another emergency power, another restriction, another category of person who must be removed for the sake of stability. By the time the emergency has become the permanent structure of political life, obedience has acquired the moral status of responsibility.
>
> The film simplifies Alan Moore's argument by making the government more comprehensively evil and V more conventionally heroic. The comic is less reassuring. Its V is an anarchist, terrorist, and torturer who understands liberation as the destruction of imposed order. He correctly recognises that Norsefire has become illegitimate. His difficulty is that he has a theory of revolution but no credible theory of society.
>
> This becomes impossible to ignore in his treatment of Evey. V imprisons her, shaves her head, starves her, and convinces her that government agents will execute her unless she betrays him. When she accepts death rather than submission, he reveals that the prison was his creation. He has supposedly liberated her from fear. The state terrorises people to manufacture obedience. V terrorises Evey to manufacture freedom. Both believe they have the right to break an individual in order to produce the kind of person their political system requires. The contradiction does not invalidate V's opposition to fascism. It reveals the danger within the revolutionary imagination. V assumes that because his destination is freedom, the coercion used to reach it becomes morally different from the coercion of the state. He dominates Evey for her own good, much as authoritarian governments insist that restrictions are imposed for the security, health, or moral improvement of the population. In both cases, the powerful person claims a superior understanding of what the controlled person needs.
>
> Present-day support for strong leaders often rests on the same distinction between coercion and its intended beneficiary. In Britain, an alarming survey appeared to show that 52% of people aged 13 to 27 wanted a strong leader unconstrained by parliament or elections. A follow-up study by King's College London found that support collapsed when the proposal was described more plainly — only 6% wanted a dictator who faced neither parliamentary control nor elections. Many respondents wanted decisiveness without dictatorship, authority without personal vulnerability, and a leader powerful enough to overcome obstruction while somehow remaining accountable to them. They wanted the fantasy of authoritarianism rather than its operating system. The imagined strong man bypasses parliament to build homes, punish criminals, control borders, or reduce prices. He does not censor the respondent, seize his property, or imprison his family. Control remains attractive because it is imagined as something exercised on behalf of ordinary people and against somebody else.
>
> El Salvador provides the harder version of this bargain. President Nayib Bukele's government has produced a dramatic reduction in gang violence while governing under a state of emergency that has suspended constitutional rights since 2022. Human Rights Watch reports mass arbitrary detention, disappearances, torture, ill-treatment, and severe due process violations. The country has also removed presidential term limits and concentrated further power in the executive. Yet the declining gang violence is not imaginary. The government has delivered a form of security that previous institutions failed to provide. For a person who can now walk through his neighbourhood without being extorted or murdered, procedural rights may seem abstract beside the practical freedom to leave home. This does not make the abuse acceptable. It explains why denunciations of authoritarianism often fail to persuade those who believe authoritarian government has materially improved their lives. Liberal institutions offer restraint, procedure, and the presumption of innocence. The strong man points to the street and says the children can play there again. The choice is therefore not experienced as freedom against slavery. It is experienced as one kind of freedom against another — freedom from state coercion against freedom from criminal coercion.
>
> The mistake is believing the coercive apparatus will remain confined to the original emergency or directed only at people the public fears.
>
> Y: The Last Man strips away the political theatre and exposes the economic foundation beneath it. Every mammal with a Y chromosome dies almost instantaneously except Yorick and his monkey. The show is at its best when it stops treating the premise as an occasion for gunfights and considers what happens to a society whose occupational knowledge was unevenly distributed between the sexes. Aircraft fall because pilots die. Power plants lose experienced operators. Trucking, construction, oil production, heavy machinery, and industrial agriculture suffer catastrophic labour shortages. The point is not that women are incapable of performing these jobs. It is that complex systems depend upon accumulated expertise, specialised training, and institutional memory. Power plant operators undergo extensive on-the-job training and must constantly balance generation, transmission, and fluctuating demand. They cannot be replaced merely by announcing that somebody else has the legal right to perform the work.
>
> The apocalypse makes labour visible. Electricity ordinarily appears to come from the wall, food from the store, and fuel from the pump, because modern economies conceal the human coordination beneath their products. The disappearance of that coordination reveals a civilisation as a fragile network of people who know how to do a particular thing at particular times. Government is not only a president, parliament, police force, or ideological apparatus. It is also maintenance, logistics, succession planning, and the preservation of expertise.
>
> This is the question revolutionary fiction habitually postpones. They Live can destroy the transmitter. V for Vendetta can destroy parliament. Neither explosion teaches anyone to operate an electrical grid. Demolition and construction require different forms of intelligence, and political storytelling naturally prefers the first because destruction has a villain, a symbol, and a visible moment of victory. Maintenance has shift schedules.
>
> The failure of Y: The Last Man is that Yorick does not understand the historical burden imposed upon him. While scientists and government officials see his survival as a matter of species-level importance, he remains preoccupied with reaching his girlfriend. His immaturity could have supported an effective character arc — a directionless man discovers that catastrophe has transformed his body into a public responsibility. Instead he remains largely unchanged. The show reproduces the cultural tendency it should have examined. It treats the apocalypse as the background for an individual adventure rather than the collapse of coordinated life.
>
> Yet its premise explains why people across generations can become attracted to dominance and willing to submit to it. The attraction intensifies when threat is joined by uncertainty and powerlessness. People can tolerate danger when they understand it and believe they possess some agency. When institutions appear unable to explain events or produce results, the dominant leader offers a simplified world. He identifies who caused the problem, declares what must be done, and treats procedural obstacles as evidence of sabotage.
>
> Democracy publicly displays uncertainty. Legislators argue, courts delay, experts qualify their judgments, and opposition parties prevent governments from exercising total control. These are protections against catastrophic error and abuse. They are also easy to interpret as weakness when the train has stopped, the shelves are empty, or nobody knows how to restore power. The dominant leader begins to resemble competence because he is at least performing certainty.
>
> That performance appeals to different people for different reasons. The dominance-oriented expect status and permission to control others. The frightened expect protection. The exhausted want someone else to make the decisions. The alienated receive an identity and a group to which they can belong. Each constituency imagines that the leader's violence will be directed elsewhere.
>
> This is the enduring wisdom of happiness in slavery. Human beings do not possess a single political need called freedom that overwhelms every competing desire. They also want order, safety, belonging, efficacy, and relief from responsibility. A society that protects formal liberty while failing to supply those things creates a market for people who promise to supply them through force. The mistake is to assume that control must make everyone miserable in order to remain control. They Live shows subjects made happy through consumption and ignorance. V for Vendetta shows subjects made grateful by stability after catastrophe. Y: The Last Man shows the material breakdown that makes any competent authority preferable to none. The machine succeeds because it gives something back. The dominator receives power. The subject receives relief. Both call the arrangement necessary, and both assume they are getting the better end of the bargain.
